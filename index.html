01 / THE PROBLEM

More monitoring.
But more accountability?

Who benefits, who gets investigated, and who decides what counts as a risk?

Kazakhstan has made substantial progress in digitalising public procurement. Yet a basic question remains: when government spending is already monitored through electronic systems, who evaluates whether those systems actually protect public money?

In October 2026, the Department of Internal State Audit in Akmola Region reported reviewing 23,605 out of 23,756 procurement lots eligible for desk control, representing 99.4% coverage. It issued 1,662 notices concerning possible violations, of which 1,573 were reportedly fulfilled. These figures suggest that Kazakhstan does not lack monitoring mechanisms. However, they describe one region's eligible procedures, and the department reports its own performance. They cannot establish how many problems were missed or whether subsequent contracts delivered value for money (Committee of Internal State Audit, 2026).

The distinction matters because questionable spending can emerge beyond the initial tender. On 10 November 2026, Almaty's Prosecutor's Office announced an investigation after a municipal department paid 202 million tenge for road-project documentation that had not passed mandatory state examination and could not be implemented. The prosecution also reported that contractual penalties had not been collected. This is an ongoing investigation, not a corruption conviction. Nor does it demonstrate that an automated procurement system failed: the case concerns contract performance, which requires different checks from selecting a supplier (Almaty Prosecutor's Office, 2026).

It would nevertheless be misleading to present Kazakhstan's procurement system as ineffective. According to the Open Contracting Partnership (2025), the share of single-source procurement in the general government market declined from 57% in 2021 to 23.5% in 2024. The average number of bidders per lot increased from 3.16 to 4.2. These are encouraging signs of greater competition, although the falling single-source share also partly reflects substantial growth in total procurement value. The figures concern a particular market and depend on how procurement categories are defined. Moreover, OCP supported the development of the analytical tools and civil-society initiatives discussed in its report, making its assessment valuable but not entirely independent.

The state has also continued expanding its digital infrastructure. Kazakhstan's 2025 National Anti-Corruption Report describes a Unified Procurement Platform connecting government and quasi-public procurement systems, including Samruk-Kazyna. Separately, the Ministry of Finance reports that a rating-based procurement procedure automatically selects winning suppliers using administrative data. In October 2026, Astana auditors were also shown a proposed AI platform with 21 procurement-risk profiles, although the official report does not establish that it was deployed. Kazakhstan is therefore already experimenting with algorithmic decision-making, not waiting for an independent AI watchdog to introduce it (National Anti-Corruption Report, 2025; Ministry of Finance, 2026; Committee of Internal State Audit, 2026)).

The remaining weaknesses are institutional as well as technical. OCP acknowledges substantial discrepancies between official procurement datasets. The government's own anti-corruption report identifies transparency risks in Samruk-Kazyna procurement and recommends better opportunities to download and analyse data. The OECD's 2026 assessment makes a broader criticism: Kazakhstan's anti-corruption strategy lacks measurable outcome targets, so completing planned activities does not necessarily demonstrate effectiveness. More monitoring, therefore, does not automatically mean more accountability.

The consequences extend beyond government budgets. Businesses may lose opportunities when competition is restricted, particularly smaller firms without the resources to challenge procurement decisions. Citizens depend on the quality of roads, schools, healthcare facilities and other publicly funded services. These interests also differ geographically: problems involving road-project delivery in Almaty cannot simply be assumed to resemble procurement risks in Akmola or less-resourced regions. Identifying where problems occur requires comparable regional evidence.

Civil society already monitors procurement. OCP reports that Kun Jarygy identified 270 violations in 2023, associated with five criminal cases, although these are reported outcomes rather than independently established impact. This makes the relevant question not whether monitoring exists, but whether external findings generate meaningful official responses.

The political-science question is: Who determines which procurement risks deserve attention, who can independently scrutinise those decisions, and what makes a warning lead to institutional action? A limited AI-assisted observatory might help, but only if it identifies credible risks missed by simpler methods and does so without disproportionate costs or harm.

The problem is not simply that Kazakhstan needs more technology. It is that detecting a risk, evaluating it fairly and securing an institutional response are three different tasks.

Kazakhstan publishes procurement records, but these capture different stages of spending and do not directly measure corruption. A watchdog needs both comparable contract data and evidence of what happens after a problem is reported.

Administrative procurement data. The government procurement portal publishes information about procurement plans, tender announcements, procedures, suppliers and contracts. The Unified Procurement Platform, developed in 2025, connects government procurement with quasi-public purchasing systems, including Samruk-Kazyna. This expands the potential scope of analysis. However, integration does not necessarily mean that every dataset is publicly downloadable, machine-readable or comparable. Kazakhstan's own 2025 National Anti-Corruption Report recommends improving data exports and public analysis of Samruk-Kazyna purchases.

These records can support indicators such as the number of bidders, procurement method, contract value and tender deadlines. They are less informative about informal relationships, supplier ownership and the quality of delivered goods or services.

Procurement trends. The Open Contracting Partnership's 2025 analysis provides a useful starting point. Its Table 1 separates three routes to single-source purchasing between 2021 and 2024. Direct awards declined from 2,326.2 billion tenge to 2,197.3 billion tenge. Meanwhile, purchases following failed tenders increased from 417.2 billion to 1,548.8 billion tenge, approximately 3.7 times their earlier nominal value.

The Open Contracting Partnership (OCP) reports progress in competition, but the categories complicate the story. Its stated 23.5% single-source share in 2024 differs from the roughly 31.7% obtained by dividing the sum of its three Table 1 categories by reported total procurement value. This discrepancy may reflect different denominators, not an error. The figures are nominal, and procedural reforms limit comparisons across years.

Administrative audit findings. The Committee of Internal State Audit offers another layer of evidence. Its Akmola department reported reviewing 99.4% of eligible procurement lots during the first nine months of 2026 and recorded 1,662 notices about possible violations. These figures describe monitoring activity and reported compliance with notices; they do not independently measure undetected irregularities. Regional comparisons would also require knowing how many procedures were eligible for each type of control.

Importantly, official monitoring is evolving. The Committee announced additional risk profiles for checking aspects of contract implementation and supporting payment documents in 2026. Therefore, it would be incorrect to claim that the state monitors only tender announcements. The unanswered question is how comprehensively these later-stage checks operate and whether their findings can be connected to actual corrective action (Committee of Internal State Audit, 2025–2026).

Enforcement and complaint statistics. Audit findings are not the same as penalties. OCP reports that administrative fines were issued in 47 cases out of 2,177 claims submitted in 2024. This figure needs careful interpretation: it is not a corruption conviction rate, nor does it establish the probability that a credible watchdog referral will succeed. The analysis also states that procurement-specific criminal prosecution statistics were unavailable at the time of publication.

The central missing link is case-level follow-up: can a warning be traced through referral, investigation, correction and any sanction? Aggregated government statistics rarely show that sequence.

Three gaps matter most: inconsistent definitions across procurement systems and years; limited visibility into delivery and payment outcomes; and difficulty connecting reported risks with institutional decisions. A single bidder or restrictive specification is a reason to investigate, not proof of corruption.

A fourth limitation concerns automated analysis itself. A single bidder, unusually high price or restrictive specification may indicate a problem, but none independently proves corruption. Some contracts have legitimate reasons for limited competition, while a model trained on past sanctions may reproduce the priorities of enforcement institutions rather than identify all wrongdoing.

Accordingly, the proposal begins with transparent indicators and adds AI only where document interpretation might help. Its success must be evaluated against ordinary rules and existing audits, not against doing nothing.

The central data gap is therefore not the absence of suspicious-looking procurement records. It is the absence of a reliable way to determine which warnings deserve action, which receive it, and whether that action makes a difference.

03 / THE RESEARCH

Detection is not deterrence.

Eight studies, three connected debates, and a major counterargument.

Research on corruption detection increasingly uses procurement records, machine learning and automated monitoring. However, the literature reveals an important distinction: identifying suspicious transactions is not equivalent to reducing corruption. Three debates are especially relevant to Kazakhstan: how corruption risks are measured, whether AI improves detection, and which institutions can turn information into accountability.

Measuring corruption through procurement data

Fazekas and Kocsis (2020) demonstrate the value of transparent risk indicators. Analysing 2.8 million public contracts across 28 European countries between 2009 and 2014, they developed measures based on single bidding in competitive markets and combinations of procurement red flags. Their indicators were consistent with broader corruption measures and supported by additional contract-level validation.

A restricted tender may signal favouritism, but the indicator is a proxy rather than evidence of corrupt intent. European estimates cannot be transferred uncritically to Kazakhstan.

What predictive AI can—and cannot—detect

Gallego, Rivero and Martínez (2021) take a more predictive approach. Using over two million procurement records from Colombia between 2011 and 2015, they trained machine-learning models to identify contracts associated with later sanctions or investigations, supplier breaches and implementation problems.

Their results demonstrate genuine potential. For two outcomes—Comptroller investigations and contract extensions—over 60% of the highest-ranked 100 predictions corresponded to positive cases. Performance was weaker for the supplier-breach outcome. This matters because an audit institution with limited personnel can benefit from concentrating attention on cases more likely to require investigation.

Their central limitation is *selective labels*: investigations and sanctions reflect enforcement priorities as well as actual misconduct. Extensions are more independently observable, but do not necessarily indicate corruption. A model could therefore reproduce Kazakhstan's existing audit preferences rather than reveal overlooked cases.

This distinction is central to our project. A model trained on previous enforcement outcomes might predict where Kazakhstan's institutions tend to intervene, rather than independently identifying what they overlook. The Colombian researchers reasonably argue that such predictions remain useful for allocating audit resources. Yet reproducing existing audit priorities would be a limited achievement for a project intended to scrutinise those priorities.

Government AI versus independent AI

Köbis, Starke and Rahwan (2022) distinguish between top-down anti-corruption AI, developed by state institutions, and bottom-up tools operated by citizens, journalists or NGOs. They argue that government systems can improve oversight but may also reinforce existing power structures, whereas independent systems can potentially strengthen scrutiny of officials.

Their contribution is primarily conceptual, not an experimental demonstration that independent AI reduces corruption. Institutional independence also does not guarantee informational independence: a civic platform may still rely on government-controlled datasets. This raises a difficult question for Kazakhstan. Can an organisation meaningfully scrutinise state procurement decisions if the state also determines which information is accessible?

Odilla (2023) provides empirical evidence from Brazil. Through qualitative analysis of 31 government and grassroots AI-based anti-corruption initiatives, supplemented by 13 interviews, she examines their functions, limitations and governance.

Odilla found opaque government algorithms, weak data access for independent groups and limited attention to algorithmic bias. One interviewee described auditors overwhelmed by ALICE alerts. The qualitative study shows operational risks, not the causal impact of AI on corruption.

Does monitoring actually change behaviour?

The strongest challenge to our proposed independent watchdog comes from Olken (2007). In a randomized field experiment involving 608 Indonesian village road projects, he compared stronger government auditing with increased community participation.

Raising the probability of an official audit from approximately 4% to 100% reduced missing expenditures by about eight percentage points. Independent engineers estimated the quantities and costs of construction materials, providing evidence beyond official enforcement records. By contrast, interventions increasing grassroots participation had little average effect on total missing expenditures, although some effects appeared under particular conditions.

This is serious counterevidence. Government auditing outperformed the tested grassroots interventions, although audit results were public and social pressure may also matter. Olken did not evaluate AI-assisted NGOs, but his experiment puts the burden of proof on our proposed platform.

Fox (2015) offers a possible answer, although not a guarantee. Reviewing evidence on social accountability, he distinguishes limited interventions that mainly provide information from strategic approaches connecting citizen pressure with state institutions capable of responding. His sandwich strategy proposes cooperation between reformers within the state and organised actors outside it.

Fox's argument implies that civic findings matter only when authorised institutions are able and willing to respond. This link is a hypothesis to test in Kazakhstan, not a guaranteed benefit of technology.

Kazakhstan and the post-Soviet context

Khamitov, Knox and Junusbekova (2023) provide essential local context. Drawing on primary data from organisations representing small and medium-sized enterprises in Kazakhstan, they identify complicity involving both public officials and suppliers and question the effectiveness of existing monitoring arrangements.

Their study challenges the assumption that businesses are always innocent victims of procurement manipulation. Some suppliers may benefit from restricted competition, and an independent risk platform could potentially be used by rival firms to discredit competitors. However, the study's evidence cannot establish a national rate of procurement corruption, and its findings do not test an AI-based intervention.

Klymak and Vlandas (2024) analyse more than a million Ukrainian purchases in 2021–2022 and show how wartime conditions altered competition. Their setting differs substantially from Kazakhstan, but cautions against treating every single-source procedure as wrongdoing.

What remains unknown

The unresolved question is whether independent AI-assisted screening can reveal additional credible risks and produce meaningful responses beyond existing government and civil-society systems. None of the reviewed studies demonstrates this for Kazakhstan.

The proposal: an independent procurement watchdog

The proposal is not another national corruption detector. It is an independent, AI-assisted procurement observatory supporting existing civic monitors in Astana, focused on verified risks and publicly observable institutional responses.

Its work would follow five steps:

  1. Collect published tender notices, specifications and procurement results.
  2. Use ordinary rules to identify measurable risks, including unusual deadlines, limited competition and questionable prices.
  3. Use AI to examine longer Kazakh- and Russian-language tender documents for potentially restrictive requirements.
  4. Send suspicious cases to trained human reviewers, who would decide whether further examination or referral was justified.
  5. Record publicly observable responses from oversight institutions, distinguishing acknowledgements, reasoned decisions, investigations and corrections.

Rules can count bidders and flag numerical anomalies. AI would be reserved for examining longer Kazakh- and Russian-language technical specifications. Every alert requires human verification: neither method proves corrupt intent.

Has something similar worked elsewhere?

Brazil — ALICE. Brazil's Office of the Comptroller General operates ALICE, which automatically examines procurement documents to identify potential irregularities. According to its current official webpage, the system screened more than 161,000 procurement processes in 2024 and supported 212 audits. The agency also reports R$1.25 billion in financial benefits associated with preventive actions that year.

These are official performance claims, not independently estimated corruption savings. Differing official 2024 totals reinforce the need for caution. ALICE nonetheless shows how automated screening can feed an institution authorised to audit.

Ukraine — DOZORRO. Transparency International Ukraine developed a machine-learning system to identify suspicious tenders within the ProZorro procurement environment. Around 3,500 tenders were assessed by approximately 20 experts to help train the system. Flagged cases could then be reviewed by civil-society monitors.

During beta testing, the developers reported identifying 26% more tenders involving unjustified winner selection and 37% more involving unjustified disqualification. These improvements were reported by the developers and should not be treated as independently established reductions in corruption.

ALICE illustrates state auditing capacity; DOZORRO shows independent expert-supported monitoring. Neither establishes that an additional Kazakhstani AI system would improve accountability.

A limited pilot, rather than nationwide deployment

I propose a six-month pilot in Astana, operated by an existing civil-society coalition with university researchers providing technical and evaluation support.

For planning purposes, the pilot would examine up to 10,000 publicly accessible procurement records. Human reviewers would compare AI-assisted screening with a simpler rules-only approach.

Evaluation would compare AI plus rules against rules alone on a common independently reviewed sample, examining valid flags per reviewer-hour, false positives, language-related errors and documented official responses. It would distinguish substantive decisions from simple acknowledgements.

Before any expansion, an independent evaluator must establish marginal benefit over existing monitoring. A cooperation agreement with auditors might help, but is not assured; unanswered referrals must also be recorded.

The bill: what would it actually cost?

Environmental and computing costs. Structured records should be screened with ordinary rules; language-model analysis would cover only selected documents.

Consider a hypothetical workload of 10,000 records, with 10% requiring AI analysis. At 4,000 processed tokens per selected document, that represents approximately four million tokens. The exact model, hardware and processing energy would need to be measured during implementation.

For an illustrative electricity scenario, a 200-watt local server operating eight hours per day for 180 days would consume 288 kWh. Another 60 kWh is allowed for additional model processing, giving approximately 348 kWh over six months. This is an assumption-based scenario, not a measurement of AI energy consumption, and excludes embodied hardware emissions.

Kazakhstan generated approximately 117.9 billion kWh of electricity in 2024, according to KEGOC. Around three-quarters of its electricity generation came from conventional thermal power plants, so even a small pilot has an associated environmental footprint.

Water use cannot be responsibly estimated without information about the host facility, cooling and electricity supply. Both direct and indirect water use should be disclosed before scaling; a fabricated litres-per-query figure would be misleading.

Potential funding could come from a time-limited research grant or a transparently disclosed donor contribution. Procurement authorities should not control the platform's conclusions through funding conditions. Software providers, hosting firms and consultants would receive payments, while suppliers might benefit from identifying unfair tender conditions.

Independence could also be compromised by a supplier, donor or political actor that funds the platform and pressures it to select targets.

Labor costs. Automation would not eliminate the need for qualified reviewers. Employees would still verify alerts, understand procurement law, examine documents in Kazakh and Russian, and correct model errors. Developers would maintain the system, while legal and security specialists would handle complaints and data protection.

DOZORRO's use of around 20 experts illustrates the human work behind training an apparently automated system. The proposed pilot would pay reviewers rather than depend on unlimited volunteer labor. It is unlikely to eliminate existing jobs; instead, it would create additional technical and review work.

Political and governance risks. The greatest danger is that a supposedly objective platform could be used selectively against particular suppliers or political opponents.

Kazakhstan's Law on Artificial Intelligence, No. 230-VIII, establishes principles concerning transparency, fairness, safety and human oversight. The OECD's revised AI Principles similarly emphasise explainability and accountability. The Week 12 research by Buribayeva, Khamzina and Buribayev also directs attention to institutional power and oversight in Kazakhstan's public sector.

The platform should also comply with Kazakhstan's personal-data legislation, including domestic storage requirements where personal data is processed. Foreign AI APIs should not receive identifiable personal data without a verified lawful basis and appropriate safeguards.

Data ethics. Public records can contain personal information. The system must minimise collection, protect confidential tips, restrict identifiable allegations and offer a correction mechanism before harmful publication.

THE VERDICT

Not another nationwide AI system—yet

I oppose immediate nationwide deployment. Existing government and civic monitoring already operate, while the marginal benefit of our proposed AI is unproven.

A pilot is defensible only if independent review shows improvement over rules-only screening, acceptable costs and false-positive rates, protection against selective targeting, and a credible channel for verified referrals to receive meaningful responses.

Otherwise, Kazakhstan should spend resources on open, comparable procurement records and stronger accountability institutions rather than another algorithm.

The purpose of the project is not to prove that Kazakhstan needs more AI. It is to ask whether AI can help make government spending more accountable. If the institutions responsible for responding to problems remain unchanged, another algorithm may simply give us more warnings without better answers.

How this site was made

This project was developed with assistance from Claude (Anthropic) and ChatGPT (OpenAI). They helped me research sources, examine academic literature, develop the argument, revise the writing, and build the website. ChatGPT also generated the website's HTML, CSS, JavaScript, and data visualisations..

My role was to direct the research, challenge the proposed solutions, select the evidence, and make the final decisions about the project's argument.

An important turning point came when we discovered that Kazakhstan already had extensive automated procurement monitoring and was developing additional algorithmic tools. Our initial proposal was to build an AI system that would identify suspicious government purchases. I questioned whether this would offer anything genuinely new and decided to shift the project toward independent oversight and institutional accountability.

Throughout the process, I used Claude to critically review claims, identify weaknesses, and challenge assumptions, while ChatGPT assisted with drafting and website development. We revised or qualified claims that could not be adequately supported, distinguished reported outcomes from proven effects, and treated cost estimates as hypothetical rather than established facts.

The final verdict reflects my decision not to endorse AI simply because it is technologically possible. I argue against immediate nationwide deployment and support only a limited pilot if it can demonstrate advantages over existing methods, justify its costs, and protect against misuse.
